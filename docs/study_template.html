01

The problem every generative model solves

You have 416 cropped microscope images of microtubules. Somewhere behind them sits a probability distribution — call it pdata — that assigns high probability to things that look like a fluorescent fibre on a noisy background, and essentially zero probability to everything else. Your 416 images are samples from it.

You want more samples. Not copies: new images that could plausibly have come out of the same microscope. So the task is to build a machine that draws fresh samples from a distribution you can never write down and have only seen a few hundred examples of.

The obstacle is dimensionality. A 128×128 grayscale image is a point in 16,384-dimensional space. Almost all of that space is static. The images that look like microtubules occupy a vanishingly thin, curved sheet inside it — the data manifold. Picking a point at random will never land on the sheet. You need a procedure that starts somewhere easy and reliably ends up on it.

The one-sentence version

Every method in this document is a different answer to the same question: how do you get from a distribution you can sample trivially (Gaussian noise) to one you can't (real images)?

02

The family tree, and where flow matching sits

Five broad families have been tried. They differ in what the network is asked to output and in what you have to do at sampling time.

FamilyWhat the network learnsSamplingMain weakness
VAE An encoder to a latent code and a decoder back, trained to reconstruct One forward pass — very fast Blurry: the reconstruction loss averages over plausible outputs
GAN A generator, adversarially against a discriminator that judges real vs fake One forward pass — very fast Unstable to train; mode collapse. Brutal on a 416-image dataset
Autoregressive The next pixel (or token) given all previous ones One pass per pixel — very slow for images Imposes an arbitrary raster ordering on a 2D signal
Normalizing flow An exactly invertible map from noise to data One pass — fast Invertibility constrains the architecture severely
Diffusion / score The noise (or score ∇ log p) added at each corruption level Hundreds of passes, reversing a stochastic process Slow sampling; a schedule with many moving parts
Flow matching A velocity field that transports noise to data Tens to hundreds of passes, integrating an ODE Still iterative — but the path is far simpler

Flow matching and diffusion are close relatives — closer than the names suggest, and section 7 makes the connection precise. Both corrupt data with noise and learn to undo it. The difference is that flow matching states the problem in terms of where a point should move rather than how much noise to subtract, and that reframing makes the training objective almost embarrassingly simple.

03

The core idea: transport, not denoising

Picture the Gaussian noise distribution as a cloud of dust filling space, and the data distribution as a thin ribbon somewhere inside it. Flow matching asks: if every dust particle moved with the right velocity for one second, could the whole cloud be reshaped into the ribbon?

That is a fluid-dynamics question. We want a time-dependent velocity field ut(x) — an arrow at every point in space at every moment t ∈ [0,1] — such that a particle released at t = 0 from the noise cloud and carried along by the arrows arrives at t = 1 somewhere on the data ribbon. Moving a particle means solving an ordinary differential equation:

dx(t) / dt = ut(x(t)),    x(0) ~ 𝒩(0, I) ODE

If we knew the field, generating an image would just be integration: draw noise, follow the arrows, stop at t = 1. The whole method reduces to learning that field with a neural network. In this repo the field is ConditionalUNet: it takes the current image x, the current time t, and the pH, and returns an image-shaped array of velocities — one arrow per pixel.

04

The conditional trick — the heart of the method

Here is the difficulty. We want to train a network vθ to match ut, so the obvious loss is

ℒFM(θ) = 𝔼t, x ‖ vθ(x, t) − ut(x) ‖2 intractable

and it is useless, because ut(x) is exactly the thing we don't know. Computing it would require an integral over the whole data distribution at every point. We are stuck — unless we change what we regress onto.

Step one: pick a path for a single pair

Forget the whole distribution. Take one noise sample x0 and one real image x1, and simply declare the path between them to be a straight line:

xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x1 conditional path

Differentiate it and the velocity along that line is a constant, and it is trivially known:

dxt / dt = x1 − x0 conditional velocity

No integral, no unknown. This is the conditional velocity: the velocity given that we already know which pair of endpoints we are travelling between.

Step two: regress onto that instead

Now define a loss that only ever uses quantities we can compute. Sample a pair, sample a time, form xt, and ask the network to predict the conditional velocity:

ℒCFM(θ) = 𝔼t, x0, x1 ‖ vθ(xt, t) − (x1 − x0) ‖2 tractable ✓

Every term here is available: you drew x0 yourself, you loaded x1 from disk, and xt is one line of arithmetic. This is the loss in train.py, verbatim.

Why that is legitimate

The two losses look different, but the theorem behind flow matching says they have identical gradients with respect to θ, so minimising the easy one minimises the impossible one. They differ only by a constant that does not depend on the network.

The intuition is worth more than the algebra. A given point x in the middle of the space does not belong to one path — many different (noise, image) pairs happen to pass through it, each demanding a different velocity. The network cannot satisfy them all, and a squared-error loss has a well-known minimiser under that kind of conflict: the conditional mean. So the trained network converges to

vθ(x, t) → 𝔼[ x1 − x0 │ xt = x ] the minimiser

and that average is the marginal field ut(x). The network is handed noisy, mutually contradictory targets and, by averaging them, discovers the one field nobody could write down.

Why the loss never reaches zero

Because the targets genuinely conflict, the residual error at the optimum is the variance of those arrows, which is large and irreducible. A validation loss of 0.43 is not a half-trained model — for this dataset a network that outputs zero everywhere scores about 1.22, so 0.43 is a long way down. Judge runs by the trend and against that baseline, never against zero.

05

Reading the training loop

With the theory in place, the training step in train.py is four lines of arithmetic and a mean-squared error. Each line is one line of the maths above.

And the code that draws that diagram:

# train.py — the flow-matching core
x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)                    # noise endpoint
t  = torch.rand(x1.shape[0], device=DEVICE)  # one random time per image

t_expand = t.view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
xt     = (1 - t_expand) * x0 + t_expand * x1 # the straight path
target = x1 - x0                             # the conditional velocity

pred = model(xt, t, pH_input)
loss = F.mse_loss(pred, target)

The one thing the diagram hides is that t is drawn per image, not per batch. Each image in a batch of 16 sits at a different point along its own path, so a single gradient step carries information about the field at sixteen different times at once.

06

Sampling: integrating the ODE

Training taught the network the arrows. Generating an image means following them. Start from noise, take a small step in the direction the network points, ask again, repeat:

x ← x + vθ(x, t) · Δt Euler step

That is Euler integration, and its error per step is order Δt2. The problem is that the field changes as you move through it, so a single straight step slightly overshoots the true curve, and the errors accumulate over the whole trajectory.

Heun's method — what this repo uses by default — fixes most of that for double the cost. Take a provisional Euler step, look at the velocity there, then actually move by the average of the two:

x̃ = x + vθ(x, t)Δt   then   x ← x + ½[vθ(x, t) + vθ(x̃, t+Δt)]Δt Heun

Two network calls per step, but error of order Δt3 — so you can afford far fewer steps for the same quality. That trade is why --solver heun is the default and --num_steps 100 is enough.

An artefact worth recognising

Those samples show several parallel fibres stacked at regular intervals. That is not the physics — it is an imprint of the training pipeline. The crops are 30–70 px tall but the network trains on 128 px windows, so safe_mirror_pad tiles each strip by reflection to fill the window, and the model dutifully learned that microtubules come in mirrored stacks. It is the clearest possible demonstration that a generative model learns the distribution you actually showed it, not the one you meant to.

07

Why not diffusion? Straight paths vs curved

Diffusion models solve the same transport problem but along a different path. Where flow matching interpolates linearly, a diffusion model typically uses a variance-preserving schedule, something like

xt = cos(πt/2) x0 + sin(πt/2) x1 diffusion-style path

Same endpoints, but the route between them bends. A curved route is harder to follow with straight steps: the integrator has to take small steps to stay on the arc, which is a large part of why classical diffusion samplers needed hundreds or thousands of network calls.

Two clarifications, because this comparison is often overstated.

First, the individual conditional paths are straight, but the marginal field the network learns is not. Averaging many straight paths produces gently curved trajectories — visible in the toy figure in section 3, where the amber paths bend as they approach the ribbon. Flow matching makes the path straighter, not perfectly straight.

Second, the two frameworks are formally connected: a diffusion model's score function and a flow model's velocity field are related by an invertible change of variables, so a trained diffusion model can be sampled as an ODE and vice versa. The real gains of flow matching are in the simplicity of the objective — no noise schedule to tune, no signal-to-noise parameterisation to get wrong, one regression target — as much as in sampling speed.

08

Conditioning on pH, and guidance

So far the model generates some microtubule. You need it to generate one at a specified pH — the whole scientific point, since pH is what controls how wavy the filament is. That means the velocity field must depend on pH as well as on x and t: vθ(x, t, pH).

Getting a scalar into a convolutional network

Feeding a raw number like 7.4 into a network works badly: neural networks are poor at resolving small differences in a single continuous input. The standard fix is a Fourier feature embedding — expand the scalar into many sines and cosines at different frequencies, turning a hard-to-see difference into an easy-to-see pattern across dozens of channels:

γ(s) = [ sin(ω1πs), cos(ω1πs), …, sin(ωkπs), cos(ωkπs) ] Fourier features

The embedded time and pH are added together and injected into every residual block by FiLM — the conditioning vector produces a per-channel scale and shift applied to the feature maps. The image path stays convolutional; the conditioning simply modulates it, channel by channel, at every depth.

Classifier-free guidance

Conditioning alone tends to be too polite — the pH signal is there but weak. The standard remedy is classifier-free guidance. During training, the pH label is randomly dropped (here 20% of the time, replaced by a learned "null" embedding), so one network learns both the conditional and the unconditional field. At sampling time you extrapolate away from the unconditional prediction:

v = vuncond + w · ( vcond − vuncond ) CFG, w > 1

With w = 1 you get the ordinary conditional field. Push w higher and you exaggerate whatever makes this pH different from no pH at all — stronger adherence to the condition, at the cost of diversity and eventually of realism.

The contrastive variant used for editing

In img2img.py the same trick appears in a sharper form. Instead of contrasting the target pH against nothing, it contrasts against the source pH:

v = vsource + s · ( vtarget − vsource ) contrastive_scale

The difference term now points along the direction "what changes when pH goes from 5.8 to 8.8" — a much more specific instruction than "be more like pH 8.8 in general". This is why raising --contrastive_scale visibly increases waviness while leaving the fibre's identity and position intact.

09

Editing an existing image

Generating from scratch is one thing; taking a real microtubule and asking what it would look like at a different pH is another, and it is the more scientifically interesting operation. The trick is elegant: don't start the ODE at t = 0.

Recall that xt = (1−t)x0 + tx1 is a partially noised image. So construct that state directly from the real image, enter the trajectory partway along, and integrate only the remainder:

tstart = 1 − strength,    x = (1 − tstart) · noise + tstart · source img2img entry

The source image therefore acts as an anchor: it is mixed in with weight 1 − strength and pins the global structure for the entire trajectory. That anchor is doing more work than it appears to. Because the crops are mirror-tiled to fill the 128 px window, the model's unconstrained tendency is to produce the stacked-fibre pattern seen in section 6 — and the anchor is what suppresses it. Raising strength to 0.95 in search of "more freedom" does not give a cleaner edit; it removes the only thing holding the composition together, and the output degenerates into parallel ghost fibres.

The trade-off in one line

Low strength preserves the source faithfully — including its defects, since a bright break in the fibre is anchored just as firmly as the fibre itself. High strength frees the model to reroute the filament, but also frees it to reproduce its own training artefacts. The useful window is narrow, and around 0.7 for this checkpoint.

One more wrinkle specific to this project: the model works on 128 px windows, but the crops are much wider. So edit_image slides a window across the image, computes a velocity for each position, and blends the overlapping predictions with a Hann window before taking a single integration step. The velocity field is stitched, not the image — which matters, because stitching velocities keeps the whole canvas on one consistent trajectory.

Symbols, and a map of the repo

Everything used above, in one place.

x0
A sample from the noise distribution 𝒩(0, I). Same shape as an image.
x1
A real image from the dataset — the other endpoint of the path.
t
Time along the path, from 0 (noise) to 1 (data). Sampled uniformly during training.
xt
The linear interpolation (1−t)x₀ + t x₁ — a partially noised image.
ut(x)
The true marginal velocity field. Never computed; only approximated.
vθ
The network's estimate of that field. θ are its weights.
w, s
Guidance scales — CFG weight, and contrastive_scale for editing.
𝔼[·│·]
Conditional expectation — the average the squared-error loss converges to.

Where each idea lives

train.py
The CFM loss: sample x₀ and t, build xₜ, regress onto x₁ − x₀. Also pH dropout for classifier-free guidance, and the EMA of the weights that is actually checkpointed.
model.py
The velocity field itself. FourierFeatures and ScalarEmbedding lift t and pH; FiLMResBlock injects them; the UNet with attention at the bottleneck predicts one velocity per pixel.
sample.py
Generation from scratch: integrate from t = 0 with classifier-free guidance and a rescale that tames aggressive extrapolation.
img2img.py
Editing: partial-noise entry, contrastive source-vs-target guidance, and the sliding-window blend that lets a 128 px model act on a wide crop.
dataset.py
Loads crops, normalises to [−1, 1], and splits train/validation by a hash of the source frame so crops of the same filament never straddle the split.

Where to read next

The primary sources are readable. Lipman et al., Flow Matching for Generative Modeling (2022), introduces the framework and proves the gradient-equivalence result that section 4 leans on. Liu et al., Rectified Flow (2022), arrives at nearly the same algorithm from the transport side and explains the straightening argument well. For the diffusion connection, Song et al., Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations (2021), is the bridge — its probability-flow ODE is the object flow matching learns directly.
